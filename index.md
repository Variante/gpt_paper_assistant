### 1000\. [RoboTryOn: Synthesizing Dexterous Robot Demonstrations from Human Videos via Virtual Try-On](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05163)
**ArXiv:** 2611.05163 [[page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05163)] [[pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.05163.pdf)]

**Authors:** Seungyong Lee, Suhyeok Jang, Minyoung Kim, Jinwoo Shin

**Abstract:** Robot foundation models have shown remarkable performance in robot control, yet adapting them to a new task still relies heavily on teleoperated demonstrations. Egocentric human videos offer a scalable substitute, but overlaying rendered robots on human videos or retargeting human poses into robot actions leaves significant visual or action gaps for high-DoF robot embodiments. To address this limitation, we propose RoboTryOn, which treats human-to-robot translation as virtual try-on: try-on keeps the person and swaps the garment, RoboTryOn keeps the demonstrated motion and swaps the human for the robot. Specifically, RoboTryOn converts a human video into a video of the target robot specified by a reference image, while preserving the original task, objects, and background. To maintain visual consistency, we translate selected keyframes autoregressively and use them to guide video synthesis with motion conditioned on the human hand skeleton. Additionally, we use an inverse dynamics model (IDM) that incorporates depth features to infer action labels from synthesized videos for policy learning. Our experiments across multiple humanoid embodiments show that policies trained with our synthetic data improve the average success rate on novel tasks, including challenging bimanual manipulation, from 2% to 60%, without any real robot demonstration of those tasks. Moreover, training with our synthetic data matches the performance of policies trained solely on teleoperated demonstrations at approximately a third of the collection cost, demonstrating the cost efficiency of our approach.

**Comment:** Criterion 1: RoboTryOn generates robot demonstration videos from human videos and uses a depth-aware inverse dynamics model to label actions for policy learning, improving average novel-task success from 2% to 60% without real robot demonstrations of those tasks.

### 1001\. [HiLo-WAM: Decoupling Long-Horizon Task Evolution and Local Dynamics in World Action Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05023)
**ArXiv:** 2611.05023 [[page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05023)] [[pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.05023.pdf)]

**Authors:** Xingyu Wang, Yanjiang Guo, Zhijing Ding, ..., Yuxiang Gao, Mengdi Xu

**Abstract:** World Action Models (WAMs) improve robotic manipulation by jointly modeling visual dynamics and robot actions. However, most existing WAMs model dense short-horizon visual dynamics, capturing local interactions but providing limited long-range task context. Extending prediction horizons increases uncertainty and computation while entangling task-level evolution with fine-grained local dynamics, obscuring their dependencies and distinct roles in action generation. We propose HiLo-WAM, which decouples long-horizon task evolution from local visual dynamics while jointly modeling their interaction with robot actions. A sparse long-horizon visual stream captures historical task context and future evolution, while a dense local dynamics visual stream models short-horizon robot-object interactions. The two streams operate at different temporal scales within a unified dual-DiT architecture and guide actions through structured cross-stream attention. Progressive inference further reduces unnecessary computation through stream-wise partial denoising, joint denoising, and dynamic skipping. Experiments demonstrate strong long-horizon manipulation performance: HiLo-WAM achieves a 93.70% average success rate on RoboTwin 2.0 and a 17.43 progress score on RoboDojo long-horizon tasks, with robust in-domain and OOD performance on the Fourier GR-3 robot. Ablations confirm the complementary contributions of long-horizon task evolution and local interaction dynamics, while temporal-scale analyses highlight the importance of balancing temporal coverage and resolution. Progressive inference further achieves a 2.93x speedup while improving performance.

**Comment:** Criterion 1: HiLo-WAM uses a dual-DiT world action model that separates long-horizon task evolution from local interaction dynamics for robotic manipulation, achieving 93.70% average success on RoboTwin 2.0 and a 2.93× inference speedup.

### 1002\. [Should the Action Expert Train the VLM? Action-Token Supervision and Gradient Routing in Mixture-of-Transformer VLAs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05249)
**ArXiv:** 2611.05249 [[page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05249)] [[pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.05249.pdf)]

**Authors:** Asher James Hancock, Boyi Li, Yuxiao Chen, ..., Boris Ivanovic, Marco Pavone

**Abstract:** Vision-language-action (VLA) models increasingly pair a pre-trained vision-language model (VLM) with a flow-matching expert that conditions its action predictions on the VLM's key-value representations. Those representations must carry information useful for control, yet the VLM was never trained for the action expert's flow-matching objective. Two signals can address this: gradients backpropagated from the expert's loss (coupling), or VLM supervision to predict discrete tokens encoding the same target actions. Prominent training recipes forgo the first, blocking expert-to-VLM gradients (insulation) to protect the VLM's existing capabilities, while also supervising it on vision-language data and action tokens. Because vision-language co-training also aims to preserve those capabilities, the additional protection provided by insulation is unclear. At the same time, insulation removes a direct signal about which features continuous action prediction requires. On the other hand, action-token supervision offers an alternative way to learn action-relevant representations, which may explain why insulation succeeds in practice. We disentangle these factors in autonomous driving and manipulation by independently varying action-token supervision and whether the expert's loss updates the VLM. In open-loop driving, insulation is competitive with coupling when action tokens are supervised; without them, coupling reduces trajectory-prediction error by roughly 25%. When both components are trained jointly from the outset, coupling increases closed-loop driving route progress by approximately 17% with action-token supervision and 40% without it, and similarly improves robot manipulation performance under both settings. These findings show that the value of gradient insulation depends on the VLM's action supervision and the evaluation setting, challenging insulation as a default VLA training recipe.

**Comment:** Criterion 1: Studies action-token supervision and gradient routing between a VLM and a flow-matching action expert, finding that allowing expert-to-VLM gradients improves robot manipulation performance both with and without action-token supervision.

### 1003\. [FloMo: 3D Scene Flow as a World Action Model Intermediate for Learning from Human Video](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05062)
**ArXiv:** 2611.05062 [[page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05062)] [[pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.05062.pdf)]

**Authors:** Jeremy Collins, Namra Patel, Ayush Agarwal, Shitij Govil, Animesh Garg

**Abstract:** Pretrained video generation models distill internet-scale motion priors into robot policies, but full video is a redundant signal for action prediction. We argue that dense 3D motion is the medium through which video pretraining best transfers to robot actions, and that predicting motion rather than video affords better sample-efficiency for action prediction. FloMo is a 5B video-generation model fine-tuned to predict 3D scene-flow and action tokens. Rendering scene flow as RGB and encoding it into the same latent space as video enables a pretrained video generation backbone to efficiently adapt to predict 3D scene flow. Co-trained on action-free human video and action-labeled robot teleoperation data, our model benefits from human-video pretraining and generalizes to unseen objects, motion primitives, and tasks. Replacing motion prediction with a video prediction objective degrades out-of-distribution generalization, while adding a video prediction target alongside motion lowers policy success rather than improving it. When motion, video, and action are predicted jointly, action tokens attend far more to motion than to video, suggesting that predicting video wastes model capacity. Together, these results indicate that motion effectively mediates the transfer from video pretraining to action. More information can be found at flomo-wam.github.io.

**Comment:** Criterion 1: FloMo adapts a pretrained 5B video generator to predict 3D scene flow and action tokens, transferring action-free human video to robot policies and achieving better out-of-distribution generalization than a video-prediction objective.

### 1005\. [DreamX: Video Diffusion Model is a Universal Latent Action Learner](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05377)
**ArXiv:** 2611.05377 [[page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05377)] [[pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.05377.pdf)]

**Authors:** Jisoo Kim, Chaehyun Kim, Junsu Kim, ..., Fu-En Yang, Seungryong Kim

**Abstract:** Learning a universal action representation from video requires modeling dynamics that are consistent across different executions of the same action yet invariant to changes in embodiment and appearance. Existing latent action models infer actions from visual transitions, but their representations often entangle the underlying action with irrelevant visual variations, limiting transfer across embodiments. We introduce DreamX, which repurposes a pretrained video diffusion model as a universal latent action learner. Our approach extracts compact latent actions from the video model's rich spatiotemporal features, compressing each latent video frame by $\boldsymbol{300\times}$ ($\boldsymbol{1{,}200 \rightarrow 4}$ tokens). In addition, by leveraging the video model's generative prior, these latent actions capture action-relevant dynamics disentangled from visual appearance. The resulting latent space serves as a shared action interface across embodiments (human and robot), environments, and action parameterizations. On LARY-Bench, DreamX achieves state-of-the-art performance among latent action models. Moreover, actions encoded in one setting remain effective in another: the latent space supports any-to-any video transfer and zero-shot action transfer across diverse embodiments and environments. Furthermore, this shared interface also enables policy learning in a compact latent action space, reducing the computational cost of video-diffusion-based policies.

**Comment:** Matches criterion 1 by repurposing a pretrained video diffusion model to learn compact latent actions that support human–robot action transfer and reduce the computational cost of video-diffusion-based policy learning, with state-of-the-art results on LARY-Bench.

### 1006\. [Asynchronous Robot Policies](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05155)
**ArXiv:** 2611.05155 [[page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05155)] [[pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.05155.pdf)]

**Authors:** Sombit Dey, Luc Van Gool, Danda Pani Paudel

**Abstract:** Scaling vision-language foundation models has significantly advanced generalist robot manipulation, but the increased model scale introduces computational latency that degrades real-time physical reactivity. Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) architectures execute high-level semantic reasoning and action ex- ecution sequentially within the primary control loop, creating a fundamental tension between semantic-reasoning capability and robot agility. To address this, we propose Asynchronous Robot Policy (ARP), an architectural framework that decouples semantic reasoning from low-level action generation using asynchronous processes operating at independent frequencies. In ARP, a high-capacity Vision-Language Model (VLM) asynchronously updates a semantic memory at low fre- quency, while a lightweight Vision-Action Expert, conditioned on this semantic memory, generates robot actions at a much higher control frequency. Such a formulation, enables having a fast perception-action loop without compromising on the semantic capability. To overcome asynchronous memory staleness, ARP trains the vision-action expert on the semantic staleness and execution delay, with temporally mismatched observation across processes. On Franka manipulation tasks, ARP achieves an 18% higher success rate and 20% less time need for execution in quasi-static setups, while 3-5 times baseline performance in dynamic simulation environments. Furthermore, ARP enables operating the VLM at 10 times lower than its nominal frequency with no significant performance loss making it much more efficient than the baseline. These results highlight the architectural advantages of modeling robot policies as asynchronous processes, enabling real-world reactive deployment without sacrificing the scale of foundation models.

**Comment:** Criterion 1: ARP asynchronously couples a VLM-maintained semantic memory with a high-frequency vision-action expert for robot manipulation, reporting 18% higher success and 20% shorter execution time on quasi-static Franka tasks.

### 1008\. [DRAP: Dynamics-Risk Adaptive Plasticity for Continual Learning in Physical AI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05029)
**ArXiv:** 2611.05029 [[page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05029)] [[pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.05029.pdf)]

**Authors:** Oswaldo Ludwig

**Abstract:** Continual learning in physical AI differs from conventional continual learning because small per-step policy errors can accumulate over a trajectory. Each deviation may move the agent outside the training distribution, increase subsequent prediction errors, and ultimately cause closed-loop task failure. This work introduces Dynamics-Risk Adaptive Plasticity (DRAP), a replay-free method that estimates the historical importance of adaptable model tensors from sample-level action gradients weighted by local trajectory-amplification risk. A ridge-regularized local linear model is fitted to expert state-action transitions, and the largest singular value of its state-transition matrix provides a policy-independent, Lyapunov-inspired estimate of one-step perturbation amplification. After each task, risk-weighted squared gradients are accumulated in a tensor-level importance memory. During subsequent tasks, DRAP uses normalized cumulative importance to scale the effective AdamW learning rate of each tensor, immediately reducing the plasticity of components that supported previous behavior at dynamically sensitive trajectory points. We instantiate DRAP on a fixed KappaTune-selected subnetwork of OpenVLA and evaluate replay-free sequential adaptation across the LIBERO Spatial, Object, and Goal suites. Compared with standard AdamW fine-tuning and EWC, the results show substantially improved retention of previously learned behaviors while preserving strong new-suite acquisition.

**Comment:** Criterion 1: DRAP enables replay-free continual adaptation of OpenVLA using trajectory-risk-weighted gradients to regulate tensor-level plasticity, improving retention over AdamW and EWC across the LIBERO Spatial, Object, and Goal suites.

### 1009\. [Do Agentic Policies Need to Look at Every Step? Geometric Abstraction for Reasoning and Action](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05058)
**ArXiv:** 2611.05058 [[page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05058)] [[pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.05058.pdf)]

**Authors:** Yihang Liu, Yiming Chen, Mingzhe Zheng

**Abstract:** Recent multimodal language models can reason directly about robot scenes, but it is unclear whether they need to repeatedly reinterpret raw visual observations at every step of an interaction. We propose shifting routine spatial reasoning from images to text and symbols through a 3D geometric abstraction that is refreshed locally from observations and read directly by the language models. Specifically, our interface represents scene geometry via typed, evidence-aware symbolic references to object parts, grounds them from calibrated RGB-D, and returns compact textual checkpoints with metric residuals after each action. Symbolic task structure can thus be reused across decisions while metric values are rebound to the current scene, with direct visual reasoning invoked only on demand. Across offline policy synthesis and continuous online execution, reasoning over grounded text and symbols improves multi-step manipulation completion while substantially reducing repeated multimodal queries, positioning explicit geometric state as an effective substrate for agentic robot policies.

**Comment:** Criteria 1 and 7: This agentic robot interface grounds reusable symbolic task structure in calibrated RGB-D geometry and supplies metric feedback after each action, improving multi-step manipulation completion while reducing repeated multimodal-model queries.

### 1010\. [GUILD: Guided Iterative Data Collection for Imitation Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05368)
**ArXiv:** 2611.05368 [[page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05368)] [[pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.05368.pdf)]

**Authors:** Shivin Dass, Kevin Nguyen, Rohan Yelandur, Junhong Xu, Roberto Martín-Martín

**Abstract:** Imitation learning policies in robotics are trained on datasets collected through costly real-world teleoperation. Often, the data-collection process is unguided, relying on the teleoperator's intuition, resulting in data that contributes little to overall policy performance. Recent work has shown that curating such unguided datasets can substantially improve policy performance, but these approaches operate by filtering existing datasets after collection and therefore do not prevent the acquisition of uninformative data. In this work, we propose GUILD, a novel Guided Data Collection (GDC) method that actively reasons about which data should be collected to maximize downstream policy performance. We introduce an iterative framework that alternates between 1) task proposal and data collection and 2) data evaluation to close the loop. For task proposal, we leverage semantic priors from vision-language models to identify relevant environments, object configurations, and goals for data collection. However, since semantic relevance does not imply policy improvement, we perform data evaluation. By leveraging predictive data attribution we approximate how the collected trajectories influence policy performance on a target deployment setting. This signal is then used as feedback to guide subsequent data collection, closing the data collection loop. We evaluate our approach in simulation and real robot settings, demonstrating that guided data collection substantially improves sample efficiency and deployment performance compared to passive data collection strategies, multiple baselines and experienced roboticists. More information at https://robin-lab.cs.utexas.edu/guild2026/

**Comment:** Matches criteria 1 and 7 by combining VLM-guided task proposals with predictive data attribution in an iterative robot imitation-learning data-collection loop, improving sample efficiency and deployment performance in simulation and on real robots.

### 1011\. [GSBR: Geometric Support Behavior Regularization for Online RL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05295)
**ArXiv:** 2611.05295 [[page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05295)] [[pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.05295.pdf)]

**Authors:** Charles Xu, Jacob Berg, Liyiming Ke, Sergey Levine

**Abstract:** Regularizing online reinforcement learning (RL) toward a pretrained base policy can improve sample efficiency and learning stability, but regularization that is too restrictive limits how much the policy can improve. Base policies -- typically diffusion or flow-matching models trained by imitation learning on large, diverse datasets -- capture many behaviors that are reasonable but suboptimal. Existing regularizers using such base policies are too restrictive in one of two ways: behavioral cloning regularizers pull the actor toward the full base-policy distribution, penalizing it for discarding suboptimal modes, while hard support constraints forbid improvements that lie just outside the base policy's support. We propose Geometric Support Behavior Regularization (GSBR), which penalizes actions by their distance from the base policy's support. Actions within the support incur no penalty, so the actor can concentrate on high-value modes; outside the support, where value estimates are less reliable, the penalty grows smoothly with distance while still permitting improvement when the value gained outweighs the penalty. GSBR estimates this distance from a finite set of base-policy samples, so it requires only sample access to the base policy. Across 19 tasks on four robotic benchmarks, GSBR raises the average success rate from 33% for the base policy to 95%, achieving the highest average success rate and throughput among all compared methods, with about 23% higher normalized throughput than the next-best RL method. Project website and open-source code at https://charlesxu0124.github.io/gsbr/ .

**Comment:** Criterion 1: GSBR improves online robotic policy learning through sample-based support regularization around diffusion/flow-matching base policies, raising average success from 33% to 95% across 19 tasks on four robotic benchmarks.

### 1012\. [LoopQ: Fair, Closed-Loop Evaluation of Quantized Vision-Language-Action Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05093)
**ArXiv:** 2611.05093 [[page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05093)] [[pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.05093.pdf)]

**Authors:** Syed Umer Bukhari, Magnus Almgren, Ahmed Ali-Eldin

**Abstract:** Post-training quantization (PTQ) for vision-language-action (VLA) models promises smaller and faster VLA deployments. However, when analyzing existing VLA quantization works, we realised that it is hard to reason about the performance of the suggested quantization method compared to existing, similar, or in many cases, simpler PTQ methods. We present LoopQ, a closed-loop benchmark that compares multiple quantization policies on the same checkpoint, layers and engine, varying only the method and the kernels. We evaluate RTN, AWQ, GPTQ, ActQuant and QuantVLA on four VLAs with more than 180,000 episodes of LIBERO and LIBERO-plus, compare against QVLA's reported results on the same layers, and benchmark whether the four public VLA-quantization releases can be reproduced. We benchmark the methods on an NVIDIA H100, an L4, and a Jetson AGX Thor. Our results show that while quantization reliably saves memory, 2-3x for real low-bit models, VLA-specific PTQ methods underperform compared to simpler generic baselines. At 4 bits, RTN, AWQ and GPTQ match or exceed ActQuant and QuantVLA, plain RTN matches the calibrated methods, and plain AWQ matches the success QVLA reports for itself. Standard LIBERO hides the losses that do exist: configurations with no significant LIBERO loss lose 1.5-4.3 points under paired LIBERO-plus perturbations. Speed is a weaker story. At batch 1 on the H100, no quantized pi0.5 or OpenVLA-OFT configuration is faster than its 16-bit model (1.06-1.58x slower on library kernels); only the L4 with int8 kernels gains (4-14%), the Thor is slower on every 4-bit configuration, and the engine alone changes latency by 2.1x. Profiling explains why: pi0.5's quantized backbone is compute-bound, while its memory-bound action expert, 63% of GPU time and 13% of the parameters, stays in 16 bits.

**Comment:** Criterion 1: LoopQ benchmarks quantized vision-language-action models in more than 180,000 closed-loop LIBERO and LIBERO-plus manipulation episodes, showing that generic quantization methods match or outperform VLA-specific methods and exposing robustness losses hidden by standard LIBERO.

### 7004\. [CRONOS: Benchmarking Non-Episodic Reinforcement Learning for Robotic Manipulation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05080)
**ArXiv:** 2611.05080 [[page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05080)] [[pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.05080.pdf)]

**Authors:** Po-Yi Wu, Djengo Cyun-Jyun Fang, Dian Cheng, Tsung-Wei Ke

**Abstract:** Reinforcement learning (RL) is promising for adapting robot policies to unstructured real-world environments. However, standard RL pipelines rely on episodic training with frequent scene resets, which is impractical for real-world deployment due to the need for substantial human intervention. While prior work has studied non-episodic RL in relatively small policies trained from scratch, its behavior when fine-tuning modern large-scale pre-trained vision-language-action (VLA) models remains underexplored. In this work, we introduce CRONOS, a simulation benchmark for RL fine-tuning of VLAs under non-episodic scene resets. CRONOS leverages high-fidelity physics simulators and adopts shared-scene multi-task settings. Using CRONOS, we show that naively fine-tuning pre-trained policies fails in non-episodic settings due to shifts in object configurations and end-effector poses; however, these challenges can be mitigated through targeted reset allocation and by addressing biases in pre-trained models. Moreover, we find that non-episodic training enhances long-horizon manipulation and improves generalization to previously unseen object configurations and task sequences. Project page: https://embodiedai-ntu.github.io/cronos/

**Comment:** Criterion 7: CRONOS introduces a simulation benchmark for non-episodic RL fine-tuning of pretrained vision-language-action models, demonstrating improved long-horizon manipulation and generalization to unseen object configurations and task sequences.

### 7007\. [PyRUA-DataFarm: LLM Agent as a Cost-Efficient Teleoperator for Real-Ready Robot Data](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05320)
**ArXiv:** 2611.05320 [[page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05320)] [[pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.05320.pdf)]

**Authors:** Kewei Zhang, Juncheng Ma, Guodong Lv, ..., Enze Xie, Daquan Zhou

**Abstract:** Teleoperation remains the default way to collect robot demonstrations, but every episode costs robot and operator time, and every new task starts from scratch. We ask whether a coding agent can take over the teleoperator's job. In our DataFarm, the agent receives a data request and one calibration episode of the robot in the target scene and rebuilds the task in simulation: it writes the scene, an expert controller, and a success judge. The simulator supplies exact actions, and a video model conditioned on real images renders each rollout as photographic video, generating all camera views together consistently. The output is real-ready data: synchronized camera views labelled with the robot's actions, on which a policy can be trained directly and deployed on the real robot. Independent judges certify the expert and every rendered episode before admission. On five real-robot tasks, policies trained only on farm data match policies trained on as many teleoperated episodes (93 vs. 84 successes in 100 trials), and under an unseen tablecloth and coloured light they keep 87 successes where the teleoperation-trained policies fall to 46. Adapting a task is fully automatic and costs \$21 of API usage on average, paid once per task, plus \$5 for a render contract; every further episode costs \$0.80 on average for simulation, rendering, and review, with no robot or operator. One hour of three-view data for a task costs \$107 on average, these costs included, slightly below a quote of \$117 for teleoperation in China and below \$290 in labour alone in North America.

**Comment:** Criterion 7: PyRUA-DataFarm uses a coding agent to construct simulated tasks, expert controllers, and success judges for autonomous demonstration generation, with farm-only policies achieving 93/100 real-robot successes versus 84/100 for teleoperation-trained policies across five tasks.
