### 1000\. [MOSAIC: Modular Obstacle and Spatial-Aware Framework for Manipulation In Cluttered Scenes](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15937)
**ArXiv:** 2611.15937 [[page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15937)] [[pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.15937.pdf)]

**Authors:** Richard Shi, Mohammad Mahdavian, Mehrdad Momeni Zadeh, Mohammed Elmahgiubi, Amir Rasouli

**Abstract:** Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have demonstrated strong performance in robotic manipulation, yet most rely primarily on 2D visual observations and language instructions, leaving scene geometry and obstacle relationships to be inferred implicitly. This limitation is particularly problematic in cluttered environments, where small spatial errors can lead to unintended contacts or collisions. We propose MOSAIC, a modular spatial- and obstacle-aware framework that can be integrated with pretrained VLA policies to improve object-level scene understanding and action generation. Our method augments the VLA backbone with an auxiliary semantic grounding task that predicts role-specific masks for the target object, destination region, and surrounding obstacles. We further combine the target and destination masks with depth estimated from 3D scene reconstruction to construct role-specific geometric tokens that are injected directly into the action expert. In addition, we propose obstacle-aware attention supervision that emphasizes obstacles near the robot body during task execution. The resulting attention features are subsequently injected into later action-expert layers to further guide action generation. Together, these components provide explicit semantic, geometric, and obstacle-aware cues while preserving the underlying pretrained VLA architecture. We evaluate our framework on pretrained VLA model using the RoboPRO benchmark, including visual perturbation tests, and further validate it on real-world manipulation tasks. MOSAIC improves the success rate by 5.1% in cluttered scenes, and by 9.7% under visual perturbations, with gains of up to 25.0% in the most challenging environment. We will release our code and dataset upon acceptance at the following Github page: https://mmahdavian.github.io/MOSAIC/

**Comment:** Criterion 1: MOSAIC augments pretrained vision-language-action manipulation policies with semantic grounding masks, reconstruction-derived geometric tokens, and obstacle-aware attention, reporting success-rate improvements of 5.1% in cluttered scenes and 9.7% under visual perturbations, with RoboPRO evaluation and real-world validation.

### 1004\. [I See What You Want: Decoding Hierarchical Operator Intent from Visual Teleoperation History](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16419)
**ArXiv:** 2611.16419 [[page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16419)] [[pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.16419.pdf)]

**Authors:** Xiangyi Jia, Kaixian Qu, Shengzhi Wang, ..., Robert Katzschmann, Marco Hutter

**Abstract:** Vision-language models (VLMs) have become a powerful foundation for robotic scene understanding, task planning, and language-conditioned control. A natural question follows: can these models also decode what a human operator intends while teleoperating a robot, without any explicit instruction? We formulate this as hierarchical intent prediction, jointly inferring the ongoing atomic action and the broader mission it serves from a short visual teleoperation history. To study this, we introduce VI AMO (Vision Intention Action and Mission Ongoing), a dataset of 6,600 episodes from eleven robot sources with 13,653 temporally aligned action annotations and 9,702 mission annotations. Large proprietary models struggle with this problem: on held-out episodes, GPT-4o reaches Sentence-BERT similarities of only 0.563 and 0.470 for action and mission, and GPT-5.6 Luna achieves only 0.581 and 0.554. Fine-tuning a VLM on VI AMO yields our Vision-Intention Model (VIM), which raises these scores to 0.825 and 0.821 and still reaches 0.726 and 0.717 on the 35.4% of test actions with both labels absent from training. Finally, as a downstream application, VIM's predicted mission enables a vision-language-action (VLA) policy to take over from the operator mid-task in a real-world environment unseen during VIM fine-tuning, reducing human teleoperation time by 34.6% relative to GPT-4o and 39.8% relative to GPT-5.6 Luna, while cutting intent-prediction latency by roughly 70% on average.

**Comment:** Matches criterion 1: the Vision-Intention Model uses a fine-tuned VLM to infer operator intent from teleoperation history and guide a VLA policy’s real-world mid-task takeover, reducing human teleoperation time by 34.6% relative to GPT-4o and 39.8% relative to GPT-5.6 Luna.

### 1009\. [STVLA: Spatial-aware and Temporal-memory forVision-Language-Action Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16589)
**ArXiv:** 2611.16589 [[page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16589)] [[pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.16589.pdf)]

**Authors:** Zhuo Han, Yeqiang Wang, Xinran Wang, ..., Wei Shan, Maoqing Yao

**Abstract:** Robotic manipulation often requires both spatial scene understanding and task history. However, many vision-language-action (VLA) policies rely primarily on current observations, without explicitly integrating spatial structure and task history. Appearance-based memories can be sensitive to occlusion and viewpoint changes, while spatial representations of the current scene alone do not preserve how the scene has changed during task execution. We introduce STVLA, a framework that incorporates features from a pretrained visual geometry foundation model into temporal memory alongside visual representations. This allows the policy to recall past scene structure as context for current action decisions. To select informative history, STVLA uses a mathematically grounded scoring rule that balances relevance to the current observation with complementary evidence from memory. Across evaluations on LIBERO, LIBERO-Mem, MIKASA-Robo, and SimplerEnv Bridge, STVLA achieves the highest average success rate among the compared methods on each benchmark. On real-world block stacking, object organization, and phone packaging, it improves success over $π_{0.5}$ by $58.33$, $30.00$, and $35.00$ percentage points, respectively. These results support integrating scene geometry into temporal memory to improve manipulation requiring both spatial precision and task history. Project website: \href{https://stvla.github.io/}{https://stvla.github.io/}

### 1020\. [Inner Product Regularization for Imitation Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17159)
**ArXiv:** 2611.17159 [[page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17159)] [[pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.17159.pdf)]

**Authors:** Benjamin A. Christie, Tianyu Qiu, Amy H. Choi, David Fridovich-Keil, Dylan P. Losey

**Abstract:** Robots that learn from demonstrations should extrapolate their policy despite state perturbations such as sensor noise, scene variation, and compounding errors. One approach to this robustness is regularization. Prior regularizers consider each state on its own and limit how sensitive the policy is to any perturbation at that state. By contrast, we explore how the policy's output shifts when given the same perturbation at different states. Our insight is that, locally, the robot should respond to state perturbations in a consistent manner: e.g., if changing an irrelevant object position does not impact the policy at one state, that same change should not shift the policy output at a nearby state. Applying this insight, we develop inner product regularization (InneR). We show that the inner product on action space induces an inner product on state space through the policy Jacobian (the Fisher information for stochastic policies), and that the null space of this inner product governs which state perturbations have a first-order effect on the policy. By clustering states whose inner products are aligned, averaging the inner products within each cluster, and minimizing the smallest eigenvalues of that average, InneR shapes the learned policy so that nearby states share a common null space. InneR enforces consistent responses to perturbations without limiting policy expressiveness, and can be appended to existing imitation learning pipelines for deterministic or stochastic policies. In simulated experiments, InneR makes learned policies more robust to irrelevant state dimensions and shifts between training and test distributions, whereas norm penalties often perform worse than behavior cloning. On a real robot arm performing three manipulation tasks, InneR raises the average success rate of a diffusion policy from 18% to 57% without additional data.

**Comment:** Criterion 1: InneR regularizes imitation policies using Jacobian-induced inner products, raising a diffusion policy's average success rate from 18% to 57% across three real-robot manipulation tasks without additional data.

### 1029\. [PhysCapBench: A Cross-Domain Benchmark for Physically Grounded Video Captioning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16424)
**ArXiv:** 2611.16424 [[page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16424)] [[pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.16424.pdf)]

**Authors:** Haofeng Wang, Pengcheng Wen, Ruiqi Li, ..., Boyuan Chen, Zhongyuan Wang

**Abstract:** Physical AI aims to learn transferable physical knowledge from real-world experience to understand, predict, and act in the physical world. Internet videos and embodied demonstrations provide complementary observations of motion, interaction, and state evolution. A foundational data challenge is to measure and preserve this physical information as training supervision. In video-language pretraining, captions express observed processes in language, yet a semantically correct description can omit the interactions and transitions that define those processes. Rich physical experience in video does not automatically translate into sufficient language supervision. This gap calls for a yardstick to assess annotation quality, compare captioning strategies, and guide training-data refinement. Motivated by our data construction for world-model pretraining, we introduce PhysCapBench, to our knowledge the first unified physical-caption benchmark spanning all three domains: third-person scenes, egocentric manipulation, and handheld-gripper demonstrations. It provides human-verified annotations for 1,500 video sequences and evaluates accuracy and coverage through seven physical dimensions. We examine downstream utility through video reconstruction and manipulation-policy fine-tuning. Physical captions reduce reconstruction LPIPS by 8.6-9.5% on third-person videos and 26.9-30.9% on egocentric videos relative to metadata captions. Training-instruction enrichment improves both PhysCapBench scores and policy success, including a 7.5-percentage-point gain for pi0.5 on a RoboTwin 2.0 subtask. PhysCapBench makes the physical content of language supervision inspectable and comparable, providing a cross-domain basis for its evaluation and improvement.

**Comment:** Criterion 1: PhysCapBench evaluates physically grounded language supervision for robot policy learning, with enriched training instructions improving the pi0.5 vision-language-action policy’s success by 7.5 percentage points on a RoboTwin 2.0 manipulation subtask.

### 2012\. [Contrastive Bootstrap Representation Learning for Label-Efficient Agricultural Image Classification](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15610)

**Authors:** Hazem Ahmed, Ali Hamdi, Ammar Mohammed

**Abstract:** Expert annotation remains a major bottleneck in agricultural image classification, where collecting images is often easier than obtaining reliable labels from domain specialists. Contrastive and bootstrap methods provide two established but distinct training signals, motivating an empirical study of a formulation that applies both signals within one representation path under severe downstream label scarcity. We present Contrastive Bootstrap Representation Learning (CBRL), which jointly optimises SimCLR-style NT-Xent contrastive learning and bidirectional BYOL-style exponential-moving-average target prediction within a shared online projection space. On PlantDoc, however, all methods fail to reach practically useful performance. These results identify the evaluated joint formulation as a promising candidate for label-limited transfer, but they do not establish objective synergy, statistical superiority, or robustness across stochastic runs.

### 2014\. [DuoJEPA: Cross-Modal Translation Between Unaligned Self-Supervised Embedding Spaces](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15968)
**ArXiv:** 2611.15968 [[page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15968)] [[pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.15968.pdf)]

**Authors:** Mahmut S. Gokmen, Mitchell A. Klusty, Evan W. Damron, ..., Samuel E. Armstrong, V. K. Cody Bumgardner

**Abstract:** Learning from paired modalities is commonly equated with aligning them: two encoders are trained until the embeddings of a pair coincide in a shared space. We study whether cross-modal retrieval and classification can instead be obtained while the two encoders' own embedding spaces remain directly unaligned. DuoJEPA trains one encoder per modality with the self-supervised LeJEPA objective and appends to each encoder a single transformer layer that translates its embedding into the other encoder's space, supervised by a contrastive objective computed entirely within that target space. ### 7006\. [NSDL: Neuro-Symbolic Division of Labor for Multi-Robot Task Planning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16671)
**ArXiv:** 2611.16671 [[page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16671)] [[pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.16671.pdf)]

**Abstract:** Planning for a heterogeneous robot team from a scene image and an instruction involves two kinds of decisions. Some must be judged from the scene: which goals are already met, how the task splits, and which robot does what. Others follow from these and can be computed: prerequisite actions, handoffs between robots, and timing. We argue that each kind should go to the component suited to it: a small vision-language model learns the first, and a deterministic solver computes the second. In NSDL (Neuro-Symbolic Division of Labor), the trained model writes its judgments as a plan sketch, which lists the goals that still need action and the intended steps in order, each with a robot. The solver expands the sketch into an executable joint plan. The model calls two tools, a detector for evidence and the solver, and is trained with the solver in the loop. We evaluate on the public VIKI-L2 benchmark and on LOCUS-Bench, a new benchmark that grades the two kinds of difficulty separately. NSDL outperforms end-to-end planners and formalize-then-plan baselines on both benchmarks and with three backbones from 3B to 8B; the 7B model reaches 97.0% out-of-distribution accuracy on VIKI-L2. Ordering and scheduling errors nearly disappear once the solver writes the structure; the remaining failures are misjudged states, and most goals the model misses are ones it never lists. Code, model weights, and LOCUS-Bench are publicly available at https://github.com/Xincheng-Tan/NSDL.

### 7025\. [Remember What Changes the Odds: Critic-Gated Event Memory for Long-Horizon Manipulation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16386)
**ArXiv:** 2611.16386 [[page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16386)] [[pdf](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.16386.pdf)]

**Authors:** Xuanzhuo Liu, Chuankang Li, Maoqing Yao, Guanghui Ren

**Abstract:** In long-horizon manipulation, a robot may need to take different actions under identical observations, depending on task progress and the outcomes of earlier attempts. We refer to this ambiguity as decision aliasing. Resolving it requires the policy to maintain memory of its execution history. To reduce the cost of processing full observation histories, recent methods build compact event memories using perceptual, semantic, or kinematic cues. However, these criteria may miss events that matter for task success. We instead propose an outcome-grounded principle: remember events that substantially change the predicted probability of eventual success. Following this principle, we introduce CGEM, a critic-gated event memory that uses a critic to select events with large estimated changes in success probability. Positive and negative changes are encoded as progress and failure records, respectively. Failure records are discarded once the corresponding subtask is completed. In a ten-subtask diagnostic environment with exactly computed success probabilities, CGEM achieves 88.0% success, compared with 0.0% for both a 36-step observation window and a kinematic event memory. Removing failure records reduces success to 1.7%.

**Comment:** Criterion 7: CGEM provides critic-gated progress and failure memory for long-horizon robotic manipulation, achieving 88.0% success in a ten-subtask diagnostic environment versus 0.0% for observation-window and kinematic-memory baselines.
